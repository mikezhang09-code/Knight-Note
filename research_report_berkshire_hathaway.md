# Berkshire Hathaway: From Struggling Textile Mill to Global Investment Empire

## Executive Summary

Berkshire Hathaway Inc. stands as one of the most remarkable corporate transformations in business history. What began as a failing New England textile company in the 19th century became, under Warren Buffett's stewardship, the world's largest diversified holding company with a market capitalization exceeding $1 trillion. The journey from a company worth $22 million in net assets in 1965 to one commanding over $1 trillion today represents an annualized growth rate of approximately 19.8% across six decades. This transformation was not the result of strategic planning but rather the accumulation of sound capital allocation decisions, patient capital deployment, and the consistent application of value investing principles refined through decades of market experience.

## Background: The Textile Company Origins

The origins of Berkshire Hathaway trace to two separate Massachusetts textile companies that merged in 1955: Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing Company. The merged entity was named Berkshire Hathaway Corporation, created with the ambition of building a dominant force in the textile industry. However, from 1955 to 1964, the company's net worth collapsed from $51.4 million to just $22.1 million, a decline exceeding 57 percent. This deterioration resulted from a confluence of factors including industry-wide recession, operating losses, unsound dividend policies, frequent stock repurchases that depleted capital, and intensifying competition from low-cost overseas textile imports.

By the early 1960s, Berkshire Hathaway had become what Buffett would later describe as a "valuable trap" — a seemingly cheap company whose declining fundamentals made its low price an illusion. The textile business was "doomed to eventual extinction," in Buffett's own words, continuously closing factories as economic conditions deteriorated.

## 1962-1965: The Accidental Acquisition

### Buffett's Entry and the "Cigar Butt" Strategy

In December 1962, Warren Buffett, then 32 years old and operating his Buffett Partnership Ltd., began purchasing shares of Berkshire Hathaway at $7.50 per share. At this stage of his career, Buffett was following the "cigar butt" investment strategy taught by his mentor Benjamin Graham — purchasing companies trading below their net current asset value, akin to捡起 a discarded cigar butt that offered one final profitable puff.

The investment logic was straightforward: Berkshire's stock traded at a steep discount, and the company was closing factories that would release capital while simultaneously conducting stock buybacks. Buffett anticipated a tender offer that would allow him to sell at a profit.

### The Seabury Stanton Incident and Control

In May 1964, Berkshire's then-General Manager Seabury Stanton made an oral offer to buy back Buffett's shares at $11.50 per share. Buffett accepted this agreement. However, when the formal written offer arrived, the price had been reduced to $11.375 per share — a difference of merely 12.5 cents. This breach of faith enraged Buffett, who refused to sell and instead began purchasing substantially more shares.

By May 1965, Buffett had accumulated enough shares to gain control of Berkshire Hathaway. He immediately fired Stanton and installed new management. This moment marked the critical turning point: what began as a "cigar butt" investment became the platform from which Buffett would build his empire. Buffett would later call purchasing Berkshire his "biggest investment mistake" — not because it failed, but because he realized that had he acquired the insurance businesses personally rather than through Berkshire (in which he held only 37% ownership at the time), he would have owned 100% of those businesses outright.

## 1965-1969: Platform Building and First Transformation Steps

### Early Management Decisions

The 1965 letter to shareholders marked the first time Buffett formally addressed Berkshire as its controlling owner. The fiscal year ending October 2, 1965, showed net earnings of $2,279,206, compared to just $125,586 in the prior year — an 18-fold increase attributable primarily to closing unprofitable operations and reducing inventory. The company repaid $2.5 million in bank loans and invested $811,812 in new machinery while simultaneously repurchasing 120,231 shares of stock.

The strategic decisions included permanently shutting down the Philips King Factory's A and E divisions and transferring operations to more efficient facilities. These actions demonstrated Buffett's willingness to make difficult decisions that prioritized long-term capital preservation over short-term operational continuity.

### Closing the Partnership and Transition

In 1969, after generating annualized returns of approximately 31.6 percent over twelve years managing the Buffett Partnership, Buffett made the decision to close the partnership. His rationale was principled: the investment environment had changed, opportunities matching his "cigar butt" criteria had diminished, and he refused to abandon his established investment principles in pursuit of higher returns in unfamiliar territory.

Partners received Berkshire Hathaway stock in exchange for their partnership interests. Those who held onto these shares rather than selling witnessed extraordinary appreciation — from approximately $7.60-$14.86 per share at which Buffett had purchased them to over $800,000 per Class A share by 2025, representing perhaps the greatest wealth creation story in investment history.

## 1967: The Strategic Pivot to Insurance

The most consequential decision in Berkshire's early history came in 1967 when the company acquired National Indemnity Company and its subsidiary National Fire & Marine Insurance Company for $8.6 million. This acquisition, Berkshire's first under Buffett's control, fundamentally altered the company's trajectory.

Buffett later stated that "it was National Indemnity that made it all happen at Berkshire Hathaway." The strategic significance lay not in the acquisition price but in what it established: a mechanism for generating insurance float. Insurance float represents premiums collected from policyholders that are held but not yet paid out in claims — essentially, free capital that can be invested while awaiting claim settlement.

This float became the engine of Berkshire's subsequent growth. At its peak, Berkshire's insurance operations generated approximately $171 billion in float as of December 2024, and critically, Berkshire's insurance businesses have consistently generated underwriting profits, meaning the float essentially comes at negative cost — not merely free capital but capital that earns a profit to hold.

## 1970s: Philosophy Transformation and Key Investments

### The Munger Influence and See's Candy

The most significant evolution in Buffett's investment approach occurred through the influence of Charlie Munger, whom Buffett met in 1959. Munger convinced Buffett that "it's far better to buy a wonderful business at a fair price than a fair business at a wonderful price." This represented a fundamental shift from seeking cheap assets to seeking quality businesses with durable competitive advantages.

The pivotal transaction embodying this new philosophy occurred in 1972, when Berkshire acquired See's Candy Shops through its affiliate Blue Chip Stamps for $25 million. The price represented approximately three times book value, and initially Buffett resisted paying what he considered a premium. Munger persuaded him otherwise.

See's Candy demonstrated the power of what Buffett would later call an "economic moat" — sustainable competitive advantages that protect businesses from competitors. The company generated $4.2 million in pre-tax operating earnings in 1972, required minimal additional capital investment, and by 1992 had contributed approximately $400 million in profits to Berkshire. Buffett described the acquisition as "buying a river rather than a pond" — a renewable resource generating continuous returns.

### GEICO: The Foundation Investment

In the mid-1970s, Buffett began aggressively accumulating shares in Government Employees Insurance Company (GEICO), which was near bankruptcy due to policy pricing errors and rapid expansion. By 1976, Berkshire owned over 33% of GEICO shares. The investment thesis centered on GEICO's direct-sales model, which bypassed traditional insurance agents and provided a sustainable cost advantage.

Buffett's involvement proved crucial to GEICO's survival and subsequent recovery. In 1996, Berkshire acquired the remaining 49% of GEICO for $2.3 billion. The initial $46 million investment ultimately generated returns exceeding 48 times the original amount, making it one of Buffett's most successful insurance investments.

## 1980s-1990s: Diversification and Growth

### The Textile Business Conclusion

In February 1985, Berkshire finally closed its last textile operation, nearly twenty years after Buffett had begun shifting capital away from the business. The closure marked the end of the company's original purpose and the complete transition to a diversified holding company. By then, the textile operations had become a perpetual drain on management attention and capital that could be better deployed elsewhere.

### Major Acquisitions and Investments

The 1980s and 1990s witnessed Berkshire's expansion across multiple industries. The company's acquisition criteria, as stated in Buffett's 1982 letter, specified large-scale purchases (pre-tax earnings of at least $25 million), proven profitability, high returns on equity with low debt, established management teams in place, simple business models without technological complexity, and clear asking prices. These criteria remained largely consistent through subsequent decades, reflecting the enduring nature of Buffett's investment standards.

Notable acquisitions during this period included The Buffalo News (newspaper industry), which demonstrated Buffett's preference for businesses with pricing power and local market dominance, and subsequent expansions into retail operations including Nebraska Furniture Mart, which exemplified the "big box" retail model with strong competitive advantages in its geographic market.

### The Insurance Empire

Berkshire's insurance operations expanded significantly through both acquisitions and organic growth. The company acquired stakes in and eventually fully owned multiple insurance and reinsurance operations, including General Re (acquired in 1998 for approximately $22 billion), which substantially expanded Berkshire's reinsurance capabilities globally.

The insurance segment's importance extended beyond its direct earnings contribution. It provided the float that financed Berkshire's equity investments and acquisitions, effectively creating an leveraged investment vehicle without the typical costs and constraints of borrowed capital.

## 2000s-2010s: Scale Expansion

### Burlington Northern Santa Fe

In 2009-2010, Berkshire completed its $44 billion acquisition of Burlington Northern Santa Fe Railway (BNSF), representing the company's largest acquisition at that time. The transaction reflected Buffett's confidence in the long-term economics of Class I railroads and American economic activity generally. BNSF operated across 32 states and three Canadian provinces, providing essential transportation infrastructure with significant barriers to entry.

The railroad business exemplified the "economic moat" concept: capital-intensive operations with high barriers to entry, regulatory protection, and operating leverage that rewarded scale. BNSF contributed substantial earnings and, equally important, provided diversification to Berkshire's revenue sources beyond insurance and financial services.

### Precision Castparts

In 2015, Berkshire acquired Precision Castparts Corporation for approximately $37 billion (including debt), representing Berkshire's largest acquisition historically at that point. Precision Castparts manufactured complex metal components for aerospace, power, and general industrial applications, supplying critical parts to aircraft manufacturers and industrial customers.

The acquisition reflected Buffett's continuing interest in businesses with durable competitive advantages, engineering expertise creating high switching costs for customers, and management teams focused on operational excellence. However, this investment later faced challenges as the aerospace industry experienced cyclical pressures.

### The Apple Investment

Beginning in 2016, Berkshire initiated significant investments in Apple Inc., ultimately accumulating a position exceeding $180 billion by 2024, making it Berkshire's largest equity investment. This investment marked a significant evolution in Buffett's technology-sector stance, traditionally avoiding technology companies as beyond his "circle of competence."

Buffett justified the Apple investment by focusing not on technology but on consumer behavior and brand strength. He characterized Apple as a consumer products company with an ecosystem creating extraordinary customer loyalty and switching costs. The investment demonstrated Buffett's adaptability in applying his core principles — focusing on business quality, competitive advantages, and reasonable valuations — even in sectors he had historically avoided.

## Investment Philosophy: The Foundation of Success

### Economic Moats

Buffett popularized the concept of "economic moats" — sustainable competitive advantages that protect businesses from competition, analogous to the water-filled trenches surrounding medieval castles. The five primary types of moats Berkshire seeks include cost advantages (such as GEICO's direct sales model eliminating agent commissions), intangible assets (strong brands like Coca-Cola permitting pricing power), network effects (where product value increases with user base, as with American Express), high switching costs (enterprise IT systems that are expensive and disruptive to replace), and efficient scale (markets naturally supporting only a few competitors, as with railroads).

### Intrinsic Value and Margin of Safety

Buffett defines intrinsic value as "the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life." This calculation requires estimating future cash flows based on business fundamentals, then discounting them to present value using an appropriate interest rate (typically the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield).

The margin of safety principle, inherited from Graham, requires purchasing securities at prices significantly below calculated intrinsic values. This cushion protects against analytical errors, unforeseen negative developments, and the inherent uncertainty in forecasting business performance. For stable, well-understood businesses, Buffett might accept margins of safety between 15-25 percent, while for complex or cyclical businesses, larger margins prove necessary.

### Patient Capital Allocation

Buffett describes himself as "learning to be a better investor through patience" and has departed from Wall Street's emphasis on trading and short-term performance. His capital allocation priorities follow a specific hierarchy: first, maintaining and modestly expanding existing business operations; second, acquiring wholly-owned businesses meeting investment criteria; third, purchasing minority equity stakes in excellent businesses when valuations prove attractive; fourth, distributing capital through dividends or share repurchases when cash exceeds productive deployment opportunities; and fifth, holding cash while awaiting opportunities.

This patience manifests in Berkshire's willingness to hold substantial cash positions during periods of market euphoria, enabling capital deployment whenpanic driven declines create attractive opportunities.

## Insurance Float: The Structural Advantage

### Mechanism and Scale

Insurance float — premiums collected before claims are paid — represented Berkshire's most important structural advantage. As of December 2024, Berkshire's insurance operations held approximately $171 billion in float. The critical distinction between Berkshire and most other insurers lies in Berkshire's consistent generation of underwriting profits. Most insurance companies accept underwriting losses, viewing float as a cost of doing business. Berkshire, through disciplined risk selection and pricing, has generated profits on insurance operations while simultaneously holding and investing this float.

Buffett explained in his 2002 and 2016 shareholder letters that float's value depends entirely on its cost. When Berkshire generates underwriting profits, the float effectively carries negative cost — generating returns while requiring no interest or equity financing.

### Strategic Implications

The float enabled Berkshire to pursue an investment strategy unavailable to most institutional investors. While typical investment partnerships face pressure to deploy capital efficiently and generate returns within defined timeframes, Berkshire's insurance operations provided patient, permanent capital that could weather market volatility and await attractively priced opportunities.

This structure effectively created an investment vehicle with no forced selling, no margin calls, no investor redemption risk, and access to leverage that costs less than zero. The combination of underwriting profits and investment returns on float created a compound growth engine unmatched in the investment industry.

## Decentralized Operating Model

Beyond investment philosophy, Berkshire developed a distinctive operating model for its subsidiaries. Each business operates independently with its own management team, maintaining complete operational autonomy. Berkshire headquarters provides no centralized services, no merger integration teams, no corporate staff performing shared functions, and minimal interference in day-to-day operations.

This approach attracted family-owned businesses seeking permanent homes where their companies would continue operating as before, with management remaining in place, rather than being absorbed into large conglomerates where corporate bureaucrats might override operating decisions. Business owners could trust that Buffett's "buy and hold forever" philosophy meant their legacy would be preserved.

The model also meant that corporate overhead remained minimal, with the headquarters staff consisting of fewer than thirty people despite managing businesses employing hundreds of thousands of workers.

## Capital Allocation Criteria

Berkshire's acquisition criteria evolved over decades while maintaining essential consistency. The 1982 standards required large scale (pre-tax earnings of at least $25 million), demonstrated profitability, high returns on equity with low leverage, existing management in place, simple business models, and clear asking prices. By 2019, these had simplified to three core principles: good returns on tangible capital, capable and honest management, and reasonable prices.

Buffett consistently emphasized refusing to participate in auction processes, preferring direct negotiations with business owners. He committed to responding within five minutes regarding whether Berkshire would be interested, reflecting decisiveness and respect for sellers' time.

The company explicitly ruled out hostile takeovers, preferring relationships where sellers chose Berkshire voluntarily. This approach reflected both philosophical preferences and practical recognition that acquired management teams perform better when they view the buyer favorably.

## Performance Metrics and Growth Trajectory

From 1965 through 2023, Berkshire Hathaway's book value grew at an annualized rate of approximately 19.8 percent, compared to 9.9 percent annual growth for the S&P 500 Index including dividends. This performance differential, maintained over nearly six decades, transformed a $22 million company into one exceeding $900 billion in market capitalization.

The compounding effect proved remarkable. $1,000 invested in Berkshire at the time of Buffett's 1965 control would have been worth approximately $500,000 by 2025, compared to approximately $30,000 had it been invested in the S&P 500. This differential reflected not merely stock selection but the power of permanent capital deployed consistently according to disciplined principles.

## Succession and the Post-Buffett Era

Warren Buffett served as CEO from 1965 until January 1, 2026, when Greg Abel assumed the CEO role while Buffett remained as non-executive Chairman. Abel had served as Vice Chairman for Non-Insurance Operations and had overseen significant portions of Berkshire's operational businesses for years.

The succession plan addressed one of Berkshire's most significant risks: the dependence on Buffett's specific investment judgment and personality. Abel's selection provided continuity with Berkshire's culture, investment philosophy, and capital allocation approach. Buffett's continued involvement as Chairman provided institutional memory and advisory capacity, though the operational and investment decision-making now resided with Abel.

Berkshire maintained its fortress balance sheet, with cash reserves exceeding $300 billion as of late 2025, providing flexibility for acquisitions and investments while protecting against economic downturns.

## Conclusion

Berkshire Hathaway's transformation from failing textile company to global investment powerhouse illustrates the power of compound capital allocation under disciplined stewardship. The company's success derived not from any single investment but from the consistent application of investment principles refined over six decades: purchasing businesses with durable competitive advantages, paying reasonable rather than bargain prices, holding investments permanently, and using insurance float to finance operations without diluting equity.

The company's journey reflects its founder's evolution from "cigar butt" investor to quality-focused capital allocator, incorporating Charlie Munger's insights about economic moats and exceptional businesses. Berkshire's decentralized operating model attracted excellent businesses and management teams seeking permanent homes, while minimal corporate overhead preserved entrepreneurial energy throughout the organization.

The essential lesson of Berkshire's history concerns patience, principle consistency, and the compounding effects of sound capital allocation. Rather than attempting to maximize short-term returns or chasing market trends, Buffett built an institution designed for perpetual operation according to timeless investment principles. The results — from $7.50 per share in 1962 to over $800,000 per Class A share in 2025 — represent the most successful investment record in modern financial history, achieved not through complexity but through the accumulation of simple, correct decisions executed consistently over generations.
